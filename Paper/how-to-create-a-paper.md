Lexical Precision and Scholarly ToneScholarly communication within the university environment requires balancing formal and informal registries to optimize readability and credibility. Text that is overly complex degrades clarity, while informal phrasing compromises academic integrity. Writing an academic paper is an entry into a serious dialogue with other scholars, requiring a tone that is formal, objective, precise, impersonal, evidence-based, and clear.Subject Pronouns and Objective DistanceTo establish objective distance, student academic papers must avoid personal pronouns. First-person singular and plural pronouns ($I$, $me$, $my$, $we$, $our$, $ours$) and second-person pronouns ($you$, $your$, $yours$) must be excluded to prevent subjective, self-help, or confrontational tones. Third-person pronouns ($they$, $them$) and indefinite nouns (some, others) are the standard.Using "one" as a generic subject is acceptable, but overusing it makes the prose sound awkward. Objective distance is often maintained by using passive voice structures to remove personal agency. For example, replacing active first-person phrasing (such as "I suggest the research is incomplete") with passive phrasing (such as "The report indicates that the research is incomplete") focuses the reader's attention on the evidence rather than the author.Colloquial Expressions and TextspeakColloquialisms—which are distinct from slang because they represent regional dialect expressions used without conscious realization (such as "Ope, I did not mean...")—must be systematically eliminated along with slang and textspeak. Academic writing must also avoid contractions (such as cannot instead of can't) and overstatements or exaggerations, relying instead on direct, evidence-based statements to demonstrate importance.Vague Antecedents and Pronoun ClarityPrecision is further compromised by "unclarified pronouns". Placeholder terms like "it" or "this" used without a clearly established antecedent obscure the subject and confuse readers. Instead of writing "This is why the results failed," authors should write "Because many participant samples were contaminated, the results failed" to maintain absolute clarity.Formal Citation and Quoting EtiquetteWhen referencing other scholars, proper academic protocol must be followed. An author must be introduced by their complete name on the first mention (e.g., "John Smith first penned..."), and subsequently referred to solely by their surname (e.g., "Smith then published..."). First names must never be used in isolation.Additionally, when incorporating direct quotes, authors must never modify the grammar, spelling, or punctuation of the original text to maintain its integrity. Any errors in the original source must remain in the quote to preserve its essence.Transitions and Signposting ConjunctionsTransitions should rely on robust signposting, replacing weak connectives like "but" with sophisticated options such as "however," "nevertheless," or "although". Overused "and" connectives should be replaced with "moreover," "in addition," "along with," or "as well as".These transitions should guide the reader through the text from one section to another or from one idea to the next. Shorter, focused sentences are generally easier to comprehend than long, run-on sentences, though authors must vary sentence structures to maintain a smooth flow.Hedging and Modal VerificationCalibrating claims is a hallmark of intellectual maturity. Authors should use tentative, low-modal language (could, might, may, or suggests) when discussing findings that are not absolute facts. Over-hedging (e.g., "may possibly appear to be seemingly") must be avoided as it weakens the scholarly contribution. Conversely, assertive, un-hedged claims made without absolute proof (e.g., "Intelligence is multifaceted and culturally constructed" instead of "Research suggests that intelligence is multifaceted...") damage the author's credibility.Category of PhrasingWeak, Informal, or Vague ExampleCorrect Academic AlternativeTypographical or Linguistic RationalePersonal Pronoun"While I was researching, I found red apples are popular." This systematic sequence maintains consistency across fields, enabling readers to navigate complex research.The structural organization follows a funnel shape. The document starts with broad context in the introduction, narrows to highly specific data in the methods and results, and expands back out in the discussion and conclusion to explore broader implications.Paragraph Architecture and Empirical SupportA robust paragraph structure consists of four sentences: a clear topic sentence that introduces the main idea, explicit empirical evidence, supporting analysis that links the evidence to the topic, and a transitional closing sentence. This structure ensures that every paragraph contributes logically to the overall thesis of the paper.Structural Organization of the Literature ReviewThe Literature Review should not be a simple list of summaries; instead, it must synthesize existing scholarship to show how the current study fits within the field. It can be organized in three ways :Chronological: Ordered by publication date to trace the historical evolution of a theory.Thematic: Grouped by key concepts or themes to highlight theoretical debates.Methodological: Grouped by research approaches (such as qualitative versus quantitative) to compare experimental designs.Chronological Sequence of Document DraftingCrucially, the Abstract must be compiled last, as a study's contributions cannot be summarized before the research is completed. The Abstract should be written as a single, concise paragraph that outlines the research question, experimental methods, key findings, and final conclusions.Structural Pitfalls in Manuscript ConstructionCommon structural mistakes in student papers include writing an introduction that acts as an extensive literature review, which can obscure the paper's actual research question. Theorems and Counter SynchronizationFor structural declarations, the amsthm package provides distinct styles (plain, definition, remark). Rather than allowing each mathematical environment to increment its own independent counter, LaTeX authors can share a single counter globally across theorems, definitions, and examples to keep numbering clean and logical :Fragmento de código\theoremstyle{plain}
\newtheorem{theorem}{Theorem}
\theoremstyle{definition}
\newtheorem{definition}[theorem]{Definition}
\theoremstyle{remark}
\newtheorem{remark}[theorem]{Remark}
This setup ensures that a definition following "Theorem 1" is numbered as "Definition 2" rather than resetting to "Definition 1".Encoding Frameworks and Character MapsCharacter encoding is critical for documents containing non-English names or characters. Dash Classifications and Parenthetical HacksStandard hyphens should never be used as a catch-all punctuation dash. LaTeX requires three different types of dashes :Hyphen (-): Used to connect compound words.En-dash (--): Used to denote numeric and page ranges.Em-dash (---): Used to create parenthetical breaks in a sentence.For parenthetical breaks, authors can use either the European style (spaced en-dashes) or the American style (unspaced em-dashes). Spaced em-dashes must be strictly avoided. Punctuation Spacing and French TypographyBy default, LaTeX inserts slightly wider spacing after periods to separate sentences. However, this can cause issues if a period is used after an abbreviation (such as "Dr." or "et al.") rather than to end a sentence, as LaTeX will treat it as a sentence boundary and insert too much space. Adding a backslash escape (\ ) after the abbreviation forces a regular word space :Fragmento de códigoThe study by Smith et al.\ was published in 2020.
Adding the \frenchspacing command to the preamble removes this extra post-period spacing entirely, which is the standard convention in many European journals.Ellipsis Mechanics in Math and ProseConsecutive keyboard periods (...) must not be used to create ellipses, as they are spaced too tightly. Instead, use the \dots command, which handles spacing automatically.In mathematical formulas, the placement of ellipsis dots depends on the surrounding punctuation :Use baseline dots when separating terms with commas: $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$.Use centered dots when separating terms with binary operators: $X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n$.In normal text, thin spaces (\,) should be placed on either side of the \dots command to clearly denote omitted source material :Fragmento de códigoThe statement was clear\, \dots \,and highly relevant.

To keep projects organized and make collaboration easier, documents should be split into smaller, modular files.Compile-Time Management via Conditional LoadingTo merge modular files into the main document, LaTeX provides two primary commands: \input and \include.Inline Merging via \input: The \input command acts as a simple copy-paste mechanism. It copies the content of the target file directly into the document at that exact position without starting a new page. \input commands can be nested inside other inputted files, making them ideal for importing subsections or complex TikZ graphics.Page-Clearing Separation via \include: The \include command is designed for larger sections or entire chapters. It always forces a page break before and after the imported content, making it unsuitable for short subsections. Additionally, \include commands cannot be nested within other included files.The main advantage of using \include is that it generates individual .aux files for each sub-document. This allows authors to use the \includeonly command in the preamble to selectively compile only specific sections during drafting while still preserving page numbers, cross-references, and citation links :Fragmento de código% In main.tex preamble:
\includeonly{sections/02-methods}
This tells LaTeX to compile only the methods section, while still maintaining correct cross-references to the introduction and results.Syntactic Constraints of Conditional ArraysWhen using the \includeonly command, the list of target files must be separated strictly by commas with absolutely no spaces. For instance, \includeonly{intro,methods} is correct, whereas \includeonly{intro, methods} will fail because of the trailing space.Multi-File Compilation PackagesFor larger projects, standard file splitting has a major limitation: individual subfiles cannot be compiled on their own because they lack a preamble and the necessary document environment. This issue can be resolved using the modern subfiles or standalone packages.The subfiles package allows each individual subfile to be compiled independently while automatically inheriting the preamble and settings of the main document. To set up this environment, load the package in the main document's preamble :Fragmento de código% In main.tex preamble:
\usepackage{subfiles} % Best loaded last in the preamble
Each subfile is then configured with a relative path pointing back to the main document :Fragmento de código% In sections/01-introduction.tex:
\documentclass[../main.tex]{subfiles}
\graphicspath{{\subfix{../images/}}} % Resolves image paths relative to this subfile

\begin{document}
\subsection{Overview of the Framework}
This subsection is typeset within an independently compilable subfile...
\end{document}
The standalone package takes a different approach: each subfile is written as an entirely independent LaTeX document with its own preamble. When compiled inside the main document, LaTeX automatically skips the subfile preambles and imports only their content. This is particularly useful for complex, reusable elements like TikZ diagrams or custom charts.To set up the main file for standalone sub-documents, configure the preamble as follows :Fragmento de código% In main.tex preamble:
\usepackage[subpreambles=true]{standalone}
\usepackage{import} % Highly recommended helper for resolving paths
The corresponding subfile is then structured with its own active document class and packages :Fragmento de código% In sections/diagram.tex:
\documentclass[class=article, crop=false]{standalone}
\usepackage{tikz}

\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\draw[thick, ->] (0,0) -- (2,2);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
The main document can then import this standalone sub-document using the \subimport command, which automatically resolves all nested file paths :Fragmento de código% In main.tex body:
\subimport{sections/}{diagram.tex}
